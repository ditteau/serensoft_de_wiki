The Year 8015

A corrupt date in a Jenzabar CX share gave us a clean test of something we usually only assert: that the medallion layers are not folders, they are a rule about who is allowed to change what.

Case study · stg_jcx__academic_calendar · DEMEAU, Anselm, Merrimack

A dashboard is usually how you find out. Enrollment for 2011–12 came back at 230 students against a baseline of roughly 2,100, and two other years came back at more than double it. Nothing had errored. No test had failed. The pipeline was green end to end, and the number on the screen was simply wrong.

What follows is short, but it turned on a decision we make often and rarely write down: where a correction belongs. The answer is the whole argument for the architecture.

The source

Four terms in the archive disagreed with themselves

The academic calendar arrives from the Jenzabar CX share as acad_cal_rec — 396 rows for DEMEAU, one per term. Each carries a year (YR), a session (SESS), and an academic-year code (ACYR) that says which academic year the term belongs to.

On four rows, ACYR was impossible. A spring term that plainly ran from January to May 2011 was labelled as belonging to academic year 2018–19. Every downstream model believed the label.

The fork

Three places you could fix this, and only one of them is right

Once you can see a wrong value, the temptation is to make it right wherever it is easiest to reach. There were three candidates, and the arguments against two of them are the arguments for the architecture.

Not at the source

The CX archive is a Snowflake data share. We could not edit it even if we wanted to — but the interesting part is that we would decline anyway. The source system is the institution's system of record. If the registrar's office believes Spring 2011 belongs to 2018–19, that is a conversation to have with them, not a value to quietly overwrite. Silently diverging from a client's system of record is how a platform stops being reconcilable against the thing it reports on.

Not in Deposit

This is the one worth being firm about, because it is technically easy and genuinely destructive. Deposit is our copy of what arrived. Its entire value is that it is faithful — it is the only thing that lets us answer “what did the share actually send us on this date?” Repair a value there and the question becomes unanswerable forever. You cannot audit a record you have edited, and you cannot reproduce a build whose inputs you have changed.

Deposit answers what arrived. Deterge answers what we believe is true, and why. Distribute answers what we publish. A correction that lands in the wrong layer doesn't just sit in the wrong folder — it destroys the answer the layer existed to give.

In Deterge

Which leaves the middle layer, which is exactly what it is for. Deterge is where we are allowed to assert something the source did not — on the condition that we say so, and carry the original forward beside it.

The harder call

Correcting and inventing are not the same thing

While fixing the calendar we found a second defect in the same table: one term carried a start date in the year 8015. It had been flowing into dim_term unnoticed, and it was setting the dimension's published maximum term date.

Everyone's instinct — ours included — is to write 2015-12-21 and move on. It is obviously a typo. But that instinct is exactly the one the middle layer has to discipline, because the two cases are not alike, and the difference is not about how confident you feel.

Case one — derive

Yes.

YR and SESS already state when the term ran. The academic-year code is a function of them, and we confirmed the convention holds on 385 of 396 rows before trusting it.

So we recompute it. We are not adding information — we are reading information the source already gave us, in a different field.

Case two — suppress

The year-8015 date

Is the correct value recoverable
from data the source sent?
No.

Nothing in the row states the start date. The surrounding evidence is suggestive — it ends 2016-01-18, every other winter term starts in December — but suggestive is not the same as stated.

So we null it and keep the original beside it. Writing 2015-12-21 would publish a date the source never sent, under our name.

Why suppressing cost nothing here

We checked rather than assumed, and the check is the reason we could be strict without being precious about it:

  • Every affected term carries zero enrollment — no student data depends on these dates.
  • dim_date already excluded them, because it requires both dates present and an inverted range matches no days.
  • In-term day coverage across the affected window is 26 of 29 days before and after — identical.

And it fixed the one thing that was visibly wrong: dim_term's latest term start read 8015-12-21 and now reads 2028-12-01.

If a term ever genuinely needs a repaired date, there is a route: record it as a human decision in a correction seed, with who decided and when, the way we already handle hold-code classification. What there is no route for is an invented value hardcoded into a model, indistinguishable from something the source sent.

Result

What the layer discipline bought

Coda

Two things that nearly got past us

Worth saying out loud, because both are easy to repeat.

A green build is not proof the model works

Staging models are views. dbt build issues a CREATE VIEW and never executes the query, so an expression that fails on real data reports PASS and then errors for every reader. The first version of this fix built green at Merrimack while the view was unreadable; it only surfaced when a downstream table selected from it. Verify a derived staging column by querying it — and not with count(*), which Snowflake answers from metadata without evaluating anything.

One school is not the fleet

The fix was written and checked against DEMEAU. It broke Merrimack, which carries a blank filler row no other school has. DEMEAU and Anselm agreed perfectly — which was reassuring and meaningless, because DEMEAU is Anselm, pseudonymised. Merrimack was the only genuinely independent share, and it was the one that broke. Check the source that can actually disagree with you.

Takeaways

What to carry into the next one

  1. Fix it in the layer that owns the question. Deposit owns “what arrived”. Edit it and that question has no answer, forever. Deterge owns “what we believe” — that is where a correction goes.
  2. Publish the original next to the correction. A _source column is what makes asserting a value legitimate rather than silent. If you would not be comfortable showing the pair to the client, do not make the change.
  3. Ask whether the right value is recoverable, not whether it is obvious. Recoverable from the source's own data means derive. Merely obvious means suppress, and let the gap be visible.
  4. Price the suppression before agonising over it. Here it cost nothing measurable — zero enrollment, identical date coverage. Being strict is cheap far more often than it feels.
  5. Leave a detector, not just a patch. The durable output was two tests on dim_term, which had no date validation at all. They sit on the dimension, where every source converges — a test on one staging model would have missed the arm holding most of the bad rows.
